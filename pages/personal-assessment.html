Individual — Personal Assessment

Personal Assessment

Inland Revenue Ordinance (Cap. 112), Part 7 (ss.40–43) — not a tax, but an election that re-computes Property Tax, Profits Tax and Salaries Tax together at progressive rates, after allowances.

1. What Personal Assessment is IRO s.41

Hong Kong levies direct tax under three separate heads — Salaries Tax, Profits Tax and Property Tax. Personal Assessment (PA) is not a fourth head of charge. It is a relief: an individual may elect to have all three sources aggregated into one computation, taxed at salaries-tax progressive rates after personal allowances, instead of being taxed separately at the standard rate on each source.

The one-line test for whether PA can possibly help Property Tax and Profits Tax are charged at the standard rate with no personal allowances. Salaries Tax already gets progressive rates and allowances. So PA can only help someone with property income or unincorporated-business profits. A taxpayer whose only income is employment income cannot benefit from electing — there is nothing for the election to unlock.

The election is annual, must be made in writing, and is not automatic. IRD will not apply it for you, though where an election has been made and turns out to be disadvantageous, the assessor will raise the separate assessments instead and note on the demand that PA was not to your benefit.

Distinguish from Joint Assessment Joint assessment (s.10(2)) is a Salaries Tax mechanism for married couples — it pools employment income only. Personal Assessment (s.41) pools all three heads of charge. They are different elections under different sections, and the exam trap is treating them as the same thing. See Salaries Tax §6.

2. Who may elect IRO s.41(1), s.41(4)

The conditions changed from YA 2018/19. Both are set out because the older rule still governs any earlier year still under assessment or objection.

Year of assessmentConditions
2018/19 onwards
current		 The individual is 18 or over (or under 18 if both parents are dead); and the elector is personally either ordinarily resident in Hong Kong or a temporary resident.
Up to 2017/18 Same age test; but the residence test could be satisfied by the spouse — the elector, or if married their spouse, had to be ordinarily resident or a temporary resident.

The substantive change is that from 2018/19 the elector must qualify on their own residence. Piggy-backing on a spouse's residence status no longer works.

TermMeaning
Ordinarily resident
s.41(4)		 Resides in Hong Kong voluntarily and for a settled purpose (education, business, employment, family) with a sufficient degree of continuity — habitually and normally resident apart from temporary or occasional absences, living here as an ordinary member of the community for all the purposes of daily life. Ultimately a question of fact. The Ordinance does not define it further; the case law is Lysaght v CIR (regular visits of substantial duration year to year) and Cooper v Cadwalader (maintaining a place of abode).
Temporary resident Stays in Hong Kong for more than 180 days during the year of assessment for which the election is made, or more than 300 days in two consecutive years of assessment, one of which is the year of election.
Not the same as a permanent identity card "Ordinarily resident" is a factual test about how and where someone actually lives. Holding a Hong Kong permanent identity card is evidence, not the test, and someone can hold one while no longer ordinarily residing here. Conversely a non-permanent resident who is physically here for enough days qualifies as a temporary resident and may elect.

Deceased taxpayers. Where a person who was eligible to elect dies, the executor has the same right of election over the deceased's total income as the deceased would have had.

3. Married couples IRO s.41(1A), s.42(2)

Separate election has been available since YA 2018/19 The Inland Revenue (Amendment) (No. 9) Ordinance 2018 relaxed what had been a hard joint-election requirement. From YA 2018/19, a married person may elect for Personal Assessment separately from, or jointly with, their spouse.
SituationWho elects
2018/19 onwards, general ruleEither spouse may elect on their own. A joint election remains available where either or both are eligible and both have income assessable under the Ordinance.
Already jointly assessed under Salaries TaxThe PA election must be made jointly. A couple who elected joint assessment under s.10(2) cannot then split for PA.
Up to 2017/18Where both spouses had assessable income and were not living apart, the election had to be made by both. If one refused, neither could elect.

Where a joint election is made, each spouse's total income as appropriately reduced is aggregated into a joint reduced total income, the married person's allowance and other allowances are deducted from the joint figure, and the tax is computed once. The total tax is then apportioned between the spouses in the ratio of their respective reduced total incomes, and each receives their own notice of assessment and demand for payment.

Why the 2018/19 change matters in practice Under the old joint-election rule, electing meant each spouse necessarily learned the other's income and business losses. A spouse who wanted to keep that private could simply refuse, and the couple lost the relief entirely. Separate election removes that veto.

4. Time limits — election and withdrawal IRO s.41(2), s.41(3)

ActionDeadline
Election The later of:
  • 2 years after the end of the year of assessment concerned — so for YA 2026/27, not later than 31 March 2029; or
  • 2 months after the issue of a notice of assessment or additional assessment for that year.
Withdrawal Within 6 months of the date the Personal Assessment is issued, or such longer period as the Commissioner considers reasonable. Where the election was made jointly, the withdrawal must also be joint.

An election is made in writing — in practice by completing the relevant section of the Tax Return (BIR60), or on Form IR76C.

Withdrawal is close to one-way Once an election is withdrawn, the taxpayer may not elect again for that same year unless the Commissioner considers a re-election appropriate. Because the two-year election window is generous but the six-month withdrawal window is short, the safer sequence is to compute both outcomes before electing rather than electing speculatively and unwinding it later.

5. The computation, step by step IRO s.42, s.42A, s.43

Three steps: aggregate, deduct, then compare against the standard rate.

StepWhat happens
1Aggregate income chargeable to Property Tax, Salaries Tax and Profits Tax into total income.
2Deduct, in order: interest on money borrowed to produce property income → concessionary deductions → business losses of the year → PA losses brought forward. This gives reduced total income. Then deduct personal allowances to give net chargeable income.
3Compute tax as the lower of: progressive rates on net chargeable income, or the standard rate on reduced total income.
The standard-rate cap applies to PA too — and on the right base The cap compares progressive tax on net chargeable income (after allowances) against standard rate on reduced total income (before allowances). Applying the standard rate to the post-allowance figure is a classic error: it uses the wrong base and understates the cap.

What goes into "total income"

SourceFigure brought in
Property
Net assessable value		 Rental income + recovered bad rent − irrecoverable rent = assessable value; less rates agreed and paid by the landlord; less the 20% statutory deduction. See Property Tax §NAV.
Employment
Net assessable income		 Assessable income less s.12(1)(a) outgoings, s.12(1)(b) depreciation allowances and s.12(1)(e) self-education, plus any s.12(5) balancing charge. Note this is after self-education but before concessionary deductions — those are taken later, at step 2. See Salaries Tax §4.
Business
Net assessable profits		 Profits of every sole proprietorship, plus the individual's share from each partnership — after offsetting losses brought forward and after approved charitable donations already allowed in the profits tax computation. See Profits Tax §5.
Only Hong Kong-source income enters PA aggregates income chargeable under the three heads. Offshore profits that were never chargeable to Profits Tax do not become chargeable by electing PA — and correspondingly an offshore business loss cannot be brought in to shelter Hong Kong income.

Don't confuse the two interest deductions Interest to produce property income (this section, capped at that property's NAV) is a different relief from home loan interest under ss.26E/26F (capped at HKD 100,000, for a dwelling the taxpayer occupies). A let property that is also mortgaged attracts the first; an owner-occupied home attracts the second. They are not interchangeable and a single property will not normally attract both.

7. When it helps — and when it does not

PA is likely to help where

  • Property is mortgaged — Property Tax gives no relief at all for loan interest.
  • There is a business loss to set against salary or property income in the same year.
  • Total income is low enough that the progressive rates average out below the standard rate.
  • Personal allowances are unused because the income is property or business income rather than salary.
  • Concessionary deductions (donations, elderly care) are being wasted against income that gets no relief for them.

PA is unlikely to help where

  • The only income is employment income — Salaries Tax already applies progressive rates and allowances, so there is nothing to gain.
  • Total income is high: the 17% marginal rate exceeds the 15% standard rate, so aggregating drags property or business income up to 17%.
  • The property is unmortgaged and allowances are already fully absorbed elsewhere.

Because the marginal progressive rate (17%) is above the standard rate (15%), PA is a relief that reverses into a cost once income is large enough. It must be computed both ways, not assumed.

9. Common pitfalls

TrapWhat is actually right
Treating PA as a fourth head of chargeIt is a relief and an election. It changes how existing chargeable income is taxed; it never makes non-chargeable income chargeable.
Assuming PA always saves taxAbove roughly the point where the 17% marginal rate bites, it costs money — Illustration 3. Always compute both ways.
Recommending PA to a salary-only taxpayerThere is nothing to gain: progressive rates and allowances already apply.
Applying the standard-rate cap to net chargeable incomeThe cap is the standard rate on reduced total income, before personal allowances.
Deducting mortgage interest above the property's NAVCapped at that property's NAV. The excess lapses; it cannot create a loss.
Bringing in an offshore business lossOffshore profits were never chargeable, so the loss is not available.
Citing the mandatory joint electionOut of date. Separate election has been available since YA 2018/19. It remains mandatory to elect jointly only where the couple is already jointly assessed for Salaries Tax.
Relying on a spouse's residence to qualifyOnly for years up to 2017/18. From 2018/19 the elector must satisfy the residence test personally.
Apportioning joint tax by gross incomeThe ratio is each spouse's reduced total income.
Missing the withdrawal windowElection runs to 2 years after the year end; withdrawal is only 6 months from the assessment, and re-election afterwards needs the Commissioner's agreement.
