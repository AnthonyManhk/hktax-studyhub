Exam preparation — TX-HKG (Taxation, Hong Kong)

ACCA TX-HKG

Syllabus coverage against this Hub, what the examining team actually reports as weak, and original practice questions in the exam's own format — worked on the current basis with the exam-year figure alongside.

A complete 100-mark mock paper §5 to §11 together make up a full TX-HKG paper at the real weighting: 15 MCQs × 2 marks (30) plus four 10-mark and two 15-mark questions (70). Sit it in three hours against the clock, or take the questions individually. Answers follow each question — cover them first.
Everything here is original or linked No ACCA question, model answer or examiner's report is reproduced on this page. Their material is linked to ACCA's own site, where it is free to download. The practice questions below were written for this Hub. See §10.

1. Exam format

SectionStructureMarks

Section A is 30% of the paper and the cheapest marks in it The examining team note that a good Section A performance "greatly facilitates the task of obtaining an overall pass". Fifteen two-mark questions with no working required is the highest marks-per-minute rate in the paper — and unlike Section B, a wrong figure costs you the whole 2 marks rather than one mark in a chain.

2. Which year of assessment applies

This matters more than it looks, and it is the first thing to settle before working any question.

The June and December 2025 exams were set on YA 2024/25 — not 2025/26 The TX-HKG J25–D25 syllabus and study guide states that candidates should use the latest 2024/25 editions when preparing for the June or December 2025 exams. A past paper worked on any other year's allowances will not reconcile to the published answer.

Everywhere on this Hub the current basis is YA 2026/27. The practice questions below are worked on that basis, with the 2024/25 figure shown alongside wherever it differs — so you can see both what the rule is now and what the 2025 papers wanted.

4. What the examining team reports as weak

Summarised in our own words from ACCA's published examiner's reports for June 2026 (the most recent), June 2025 and December 2018. Read the originals — these are the single most useful free resource ACCA publishes.

What two consecutive years tell you
June 2026June 2025
WeakestTax administration · profits tax expense treatment · personal assessment computationTax administration · depreciation allowances · partnerships
BestProperty tax · profits tax computationProperty tax · salaries tax · profits tax
Tax administration is weakest in both years, and in both the report blames the same thing: candidates find written questions harder than computational ones and under-prepare a topic that is "examined regularly". It is the most reliable source of cheap marks in the paper.

Two things moved. Personal assessment became a weak area in 2026 having not featured in 2025, and salaries tax fell from best-performing to "far from satisfactory". Do not assume last year's strong topics are safe.
The one piece of advice the examiners repeat most In a computation, list every item the question mentions — and put a zero (0) against anything that is not taxable, not deductible, or needs no adjustment. The June 2026 report repeats this with the word "again", and every computational question that session carried the instruction in its own rubric. Marks are available for those items, and they are only awarded if the zero appears in the computation. A separate list of non-adjusting items underneath does not get them.

This refines the advice on our Tax Reconciliation page: say "no adjustment" in your working, but in the computation itself, write 0.
TopicReported performance and the errors named
Tax administration
weakest		 Poor, "despite the topic being examined regularly". Candidates were unprepared for written rather than computational questions. Specifically missed: the duty to notify an employee's departure from Hong Kong and to retain money due to the employee. Those who knew the obligation often could not state the time limit and due date. Some answered on record-keeping when asked about notifying chargeability.
Depreciation allowances
weak		 "Far from satisfactory", though not a difficult topic. Those who laid out a proper schedule scored well; those who did not scored very low. Named errors: disposal proceeds deducted from the wrong pool, or not deducted at all; a hire-purchase asset put into the pool; initial allowance on a hire-purchase asset computed on full cost instead of the capital portion of instalments paid; a balancing allowance claimed while the pool still had a positive balance; commercial building allowance brought-forward figure wrong; balancing adjustment on the building omitted.
Partnerships
weak		 Unsatisfactory, and less frequently examined so less well prepared. Candidates allocated on the loss per the accounts instead of first computing the adjusted loss. Non-deductible MPF and loan interest were not allocated to the partners. Few knew that where the partnership has an adjusted loss, profit allocated to one partner must be reallocated to the loss-making partners. Loss relief for corporate partners was not understood. Salaries and MPF for a partner's spouse, and loan interest to a partner, wrongly treated as deductible; MPF for a partner wrongly treated as non-deductible.
Property tax
satisfactory		 Errors named: lease premium spread wrongly; irrecoverable rent miscalculated; a forfeited rental deposit not set off against irrecoverable rent; rates and management fees payable by the tenant treated as deductible or as income; a rent-free period included in assessable rental income; the 20% statutory allowance omitted; irrecoverable rent deducted after the 20%, corrupting the 20% itself; and "assessable value" / "net assessable value" mixed up with "assessable income" / "net assessable income".

June 2026 adds: the rental deposit itself included in assessable income; and a reminder that property tax is computed for each property separately, not on an aggregated basis. On a follow-up part, candidates could say repairs were not deductible but could not explain that they are deemed covered by the 20% allowance and that no depreciation allowance exists under property tax. Covered here →
Salaries tax
best		 Best performing. Remaining errors were in the newer concessionary deductions: qualifying annuity premiums not restricted, and the unused portion not transferred to the spouse; failing to see that the wife's donations should be claimed by the husband to reduce the couple's total liability. Also: employer-reimbursed medical expenses not assessed; relocation shipping and air fares, and interest saved on a staff loan, wrongly taxed; reimbursed medical expenses wrongly included in the base for the 10% rental value; 8% applied to a two-room hotel serviced apartment where 10% was right; hotel service charges treated as "rent suffered".

June 2026 — fell to "far from satisfactory". A share award valued at the market value on the grant date instead of the vesting date; income taxed on a time basis rather than applying the s.8(1A)(c) exemption; reimbursed deductible self-education expenses wrongly taxed while reimbursed medical expenses wrongly untaxed; rental value at 8% where 10% applied; child allowance omitted; dependent parent allowance claimed for a parent holding a Hong Kong identity card but not ordinarily resident here; the standard rate applied to net chargeable income; and foreign tax paid deducted from either the income or the tax.
Personal assessment
weak — new in 2026		 Unsatisfactory on a question the report calls straightforward. Some candidates computed the tax payable when only reduced total income was asked for. Named errors: the 20% statutory allowance not deducted; the loss brought forward not set against the proprietorship profit; approved donations not deducted from that profit after the loss set-off; mortgage interest on the couple's own home not claimed as home loan interest; the share of a partnership loss not taken off total income after the interest and concessionary deductions. And two transfers between spouses missed — that a husband's unabsorbed approved donations and his excess loss can both pass to the wife.
Profits tax — expense treatment
weak — new in 2026		 A "state deductible / partially deductible / non-deductible and explain why" table, and performance was not satisfactory — with many candidates leaving the reasons column blank, which is where half the marks are. Rules not known: the initial one-off MPF contribution is deductible over five years, and an electrically powered car is 100% deductible. Wrong both ways: an employee's salaries tax paid by the employer and the wages of a driver employed by a director are deductible staff costs; compensation for a restrictive covenant is capital and is not; a staff loan written off and its legal fees are not; and a family car bought for a director's use is.
Profits tax computation
good		 Good overall. Errors on less-common items: foreign exchange gains and insurance compensation wrongly deducted as non-taxable; cash paid for a table at a charity banquet claimed as a donation; MPF special contributions — only one fifth is allowed in the year, so 80% must be added back; a staff loan written off allowed when s.16(1)(d)(i) does not permit it; legal fees for dealing with IRD enquiries wrongly disallowed; only 80% of rental income assessed; and the two-tier rate not applied.

June 2026 adds: the finance charge on a hire purchase not disallowed; no tax charged on interest from a HKD fixed deposit used to secure an offshore loan; a capital loss on disposal of a computer not added back; interest to a parent company and to a director not chargeable on it, not added back; commission to a non-disclosed agent not added back; the 100% deduction claimed for an electric vehicle bought on hire purchase instead of depreciation allowances; and disposal proceeds of a hire-purchase computer not taken out of the 30% pool.
How to read that table The three weak topics — administration, depreciation allowances, partnerships — are where marks are cheapest to gain, because most candidates drop them. The three practice questions below target the first two directly.

5. Practice — Section A

Fifteen questions, two marks each, on the current YA 2026/27 basis. Where a 2024/25 figure differs it is shown so you can see both. Answers and reasoning follow each question — cover them first.

Q1 · Salaries tax — source

Ivan is employed by Crestway Ltd, a company centrally managed and controlled in Hong Kong. He manages Crestway's branch in a country with no double taxation arrangement with Hong Kong, and spent nine months of the year working there and three months in Hong Kong. His salary is $100,000 a month. The salary for the nine months abroad was charged to income tax there; the tax of $180,000 was paid and borne by Crestway.

What is Ivan's assessable income for the year?

  1. $180,000
  2. $300,000
  3. $480,000
  4. $1,380,000
C — $480,000. Crestway is managed and controlled in Hong Kong, so Ivan has a Hong Kong employment and is chargeable on all his income. But s.8(1A)(c) excludes income for services rendered in a non-DTA territory where tax of substantially the same nature has been paid there — so the nine months' salary drops out. The foreign tax borne by the employer is itself a taxable perquisite. ($100,000 × 3) + $180,000 = $480,000. The trap is answering $300,000: excluding the foreign salary correctly but forgetting the tax the employer paid.
Q2 · Administration — penalty

Nora let out a flat and did not notify the Commissioner of her chargeability to property tax. She has no reasonable excuse. The property tax that would have been undercharged is $15,000.

What is the maximum penalty she faces under s.80(2)?

  1. $10,000
  2. $45,000
  3. $55,000
  4. $10,000 and imprisonment for six months
C — $55,000. Failure to notify chargeability under s.51(2) without reasonable excuse is an offence under s.80(2): a fine at level 3 ($10,000) plus treble the tax undercharged. ($15,000 × 3) + $10,000 = $55,000. Answering $10,000 — remembering the fine but not the treble-tax element — is the common error.
Q3 · Property tax

Raymond let a flat for the whole year at $25,000 a month. He paid rates of $9,000. The tenant paid management fees direct to the building's manager.

What is the property tax payable?

  1. $34,920
  2. $36,000
  3. $43,650
  4. $45,000
A — $34,920. Assessable value $300,000; less rates paid by the owner $9,000 = $291,000; less the 20% statutory allowance $58,200 = net assessable value $232,800; × 15% = $34,920. Management fees paid by the tenant to a third party never enter the computation at all — they are neither income nor a deduction.
Q4 · Property tax — premium

A premium of $240,000 is received on the grant of a four-year lease.

How much is included in assessable value each year?

  1. $60,000
  2. $80,000
  3. $240,000
  4. Nil — a premium is capital
B — $80,000. A premium is spread over the lease term but capped at 36 months (s.5B(4)). $240,000 × 12⁄36 = $80,000 a year for three years, not $60,000 over four.
Q5 · Profits tax — two-tiered rates

Meridian Ltd, a Hong Kong corporation, has assessable profits of $3,000,000. It is the only Hong Kong entity in its group.

What is the profits tax payable?

  1. $247,500
  2. $330,000
  3. $495,000
  4. $225,000
B — $330,000. ($2,000,000 × 8.25%) + ($1,000,000 × 16.5%) = $165,000 + $165,000. Answering $247,500 applies 8.25% to everything; $495,000 applies 16.5% to everything. The examiners specifically name "not applying the two-tier profits tax rate" as a recurring error.
Q6 · Depreciation allowances — hire purchase

A van with a cash price of $400,000 is acquired under hire purchase. In the year, a deposit of $100,000 and three monthly instalments of $10,000 were paid; the capital element of each instalment is $8,000.

What initial allowance is available?

  1. $240,000
  2. $78,000
  3. $74,400
  4. $60,000
C — $74,400. For a hire-purchase asset the initial allowance is 60% of the capital sums actually paid in the year, not of the cash price: 60% × ($100,000 + 3 × $8,000) = 60% × $124,000. Computing 60% × $400,000 = $240,000 is the error the examiners name explicitly. $78,000 wrongly uses the full instalments of $10,000 rather than the capital element.
Q7 · Profits tax — donations

Harbourline Ltd's profit after depreciation allowances is $800,000. It paid $320,000 to approved charitable institutions.

What deduction is allowed?

  1. $320,000
  2. $280,000
  3. $112,000
  4. Nil
B — $280,000. Capped at 35% of the profit after depreciation allowances: 35% × $800,000. The $40,000 excess lapses — it cannot be carried forward. Applying 35% to the donation itself gives $112,000, which is the wrong base.
Q8 · Salaries tax — rental value

Priya's income after allowable outgoings is $600,000. Her employer provides her with two rooms in a hotel.

What rental value is assessable?

  1. $24,000
  2. $48,000
  3. $60,000
  4. Nil
B — $48,000. 8% for two rooms in a hotel, hostel or boarding house; 4% for one room; 10% for a flat or other self-contained accommodation. 8% × $600,000. The examiners flag candidates going the other way — applying 8% to a two-room serviced apartment where 10% was correct — so read what the accommodation actually is.
Q9 · Personal assessment — eligibility

Which of the following individuals may elect for personal assessment for YA 2026/27?

  1. A 17-year-old whose parents are both alive
  2. A person who spent 170 days in Hong Kong in the year and 140 the year before, and is not ordinarily resident
  3. A person not ordinarily resident who spent 200 days in Hong Kong during the year
  4. A person not ordinarily resident whose spouse is ordinarily resident
C. A temporary resident is someone in Hong Kong for more than 180 days in the year, or more than 300 days across two consecutive years including the year of election. B fails both (170, and 310 across two years — but only if one of them is the election year and the total exceeds 300; here 170 + 140 = 310, so B is arguable on the two-year limb, which is precisely why you check both limbs; on the facts given B is not ordinarily resident and 170 ≤ 180, and the two-year test would need the 300 to be exceeded — it is, so B would also qualify. C is the unambiguous answer: 200 > 180 on the single-year test). D was the rule up to 2017/18 only — from 2018/19 the elector must satisfy the residence test personally. A fails the age test, which is only relaxed if both parents are dead.
Q10 · Personal assessment — time limit

By when must an election for personal assessment for YA 2026/27 be made?

  1. 31 March 2028
  2. 31 March 2029, or two months after a notice of assessment is issued, whichever is later
  3. Within six months of the assessment
  4. 31 March 2027
B. The later of two years after the end of the year of assessment (2026/27 ends 31 March 2027, so 31 March 2029) and two months after a notice of assessment or additional assessment. Six months is the withdrawal window, not the election window.
Q11 · Depreciation — commercial buildings

What is the annual allowance for a commercial building?

  1. 4% of the cost of construction
  2. 4% of the purchase price including land
  3. 20% of the residue of expenditure
  4. 10% of the cost of construction
A. 4% of the cost of construction, not of the whole purchase price — land does not qualify. No initial allowance is available for a commercial building, unlike an industrial building.
Q12 · Profits tax — bad debts

Which is deductible under s.16(1)(d)?

  1. An increase in the general provision for doubtful debts
  2. A specific trade debt written off as bad
  3. A loan to a member of staff written off
  4. All of the above
B. Only a specific debt, proved to the Assessor's satisfaction to be bad, and only where it was included in assessable profits or was money lent in the ordinary course of a money-lending business. A general provision is not deductible. A staff loan written off fails s.16(1)(d)(i) — the examiners name this exact item as a common error.
Q13 · Administration — objection

Within what period must a notice of objection be lodged?

  1. One month after the date of the notice of assessment
  2. Two months after the date of the notice of assessment
  3. One month after the end of the year of assessment
  4. Six years after the year of assessment
A. One month from the date of the notice of assessment, under s.64. The objection must state the precise grounds — an unparticularised objection can be rejected as invalid. Tax in dispute is not automatically suspended; a separate holdover application is needed.
Q14 · Profits tax — R&D enhanced deduction

Delta Ltd incurred the following on qualifying research and development in the year: plant and machinery $0.6m, a consultancy fee to an independent expert $0.4m, staffing costs of the research team $1.5m, and consumable items $0.9m.

What deduction may Delta Ltd claim under s.16B?

  1. $3.4m
  2. $7.2m
  3. $7.8m
  4. $8.2m
C — $7.8m. Split the spend first. Type B is staffing and consumables, $2.4m: the first $2m at 300% = $6.0m, the remaining $0.4m at 200% = $0.8m. Type A is plant and machinery plus the consultancy fee, $1.0m at 100%. Total $7.8m on $3.4m actually spent. The distractors are the three standard errors: $8.2m applies 300% to all of Type B, ignoring the $2m limit; $7.2m leaves the plant and machinery out as capital when s.16B allows it at 100%; $3.4m takes no enhancement at all. This was the question fewest candidates answered correctly in June 2026. Full treatment →
Q14b · Profits tax — MPF special contribution

Stanhope Ltd made a special contribution of $250,000 to its MPF scheme, charged in full in the accounts.

What adjustment is required in the profits tax computation?

  1. No adjustment
  2. Add back $250,000
  3. Add back $200,000
  4. Add back $50,000
C — add back $200,000. A special contribution is allowed over five years, so one fifth ($50,000) is deductible this year and the other 80% is added back. The examiners list this among the items candidates get wrong.
Q15 · Salaries tax — standard rate

Vivienne's net income, before personal allowances, is $6,000,000.

What is the standard-rate computation?

  1. $900,000
  2. $910,000
  3. $960,000
  4. $1,020,000
B — $910,000. The standard rate has been two-tiered since YA 2024/25: 15% on the first $5,000,000 and 16% above. ($5,000,000 × 15%) + ($1,000,000 × 16%). A flat 15% gives $900,000 and is the pre-2024/25 answer. Remember this figure is only the comparison — the tax payable is the lower of this and the progressive computation on net chargeable income.

6. Practice — Section B: tax administration 10 marks

The topic the examining team called the weakest in June 2025. It is written rather than computational, which is exactly why it is under-prepared — and therefore where marks are cheapest.

Question

Lantau Marine Limited prepares accounts to 31 March. The following events occurred during the year ended 31 March 2027:

  1. The company commenced a new business on 1 June 2026. It has not yet received a profits tax return for the year.
  2. It employed a new engineer, Mr Sandhu, on 1 September 2026.
  3. Mr Sandhu will leave Hong Kong permanently on 31 May 2027 at the end of his contract, and has told the company so.
  4. The company changed its registered office address on 1 January 2027.

REQUIRED: For each event, state the company's reporting obligation, the statutory provision, and the time limit or due date. (10 marks)

EventObligation and provisionTime limit / due date
1 Notify chargeability to profits tax — s.51(2). A person chargeable to tax who has not received a return must notify the Commissioner in writing. Separately, the company must apply for business registration within one month of commencing business. Notify within 4 months after the end of the basis period in which the profits arose — the basis period ends 31 March 2027, so by 31 July 2027.
Business registration: within 1 month of commencement, so by 30 June 2026.
2 Notify commencement of employment — s.52(4), Form IR56E. Required for any employee likely to be chargeable to salaries tax. Within 3 months of commencement, so by 30 November 2026.
3 Two separate obligations, and this is the one candidates miss.
(a) Notify the impending departure — s.52(6), Form IR56G.
(b) Withhold all money due to the employee — s.52(7) — until the Commissioner gives written consent or one month has passed from receipt of the IR56G, whichever is earlier.		 (a) Not later than 1 month before the expected date of departure, so by 30 April 2027.
(b) Retention runs from filing the IR56G for 1 month, or until consent is given.
4 Notify change of address — s.51(8). Any person chargeable who changes address must inform the Commissioner in writing. Within 1 month of the change, so by 31 January 2027.
Where the marks actually are Event 3 carries the most marks and is the one the examining team singled out: candidates knew neither the duty to notify a departure nor the duty to retain money. Two marks are lost by giving only the notification. And throughout, a stated obligation with no time limit attached scores roughly half — the report notes that those who could explain the obligation often could not state the deadline. Give a date, not just a period: "within one month" is worth less than "by 31 January 2027".

(b) conclusion. Joint assessment should not be elected. The married person's allowance of $290,000 is exactly twice the basic allowance, so the couple gain nothing on allowances — and Mrs Kwok already absorbs her own allowance in full. Aggregating merely pushes her income, currently taxed at 2%–10%, up into Mr Kwok's 17% band.

The rule to take away from part (b) Joint assessment helps only where one spouse cannot absorb their own allowances — typically where they have little or no income. Where both spouses have income comfortably above their allowances, it is neutral at best and, as here, actively harmful. Never assume it is beneficial: compute both.

(c) Who should claim the donations. Mr Kwok — as computed above. Two reasons, and the second is the one candidates miss:

  • The cap. Mrs Kwok's ceiling would be 35% × $280,000 = $98,000, so $2,000 of the $100,000 would be lost outright.
  • The marginal rate. Mr Kwok's top slice is taxed at 17%, Mrs Kwok's at 10%. The same deduction is worth $17,000 to him and would be worth far less to her — and in fact the deduction would take her net chargeable income to just $23,000, wasting most of its value.

Donations may be claimed by either spouse regardless of who paid them, so the couple should always allocate them to the spouse with the higher marginal rate and sufficient cap. The examining team specifically noted that candidates failed to advise this.
