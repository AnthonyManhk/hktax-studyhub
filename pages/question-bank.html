  1. No - the marginal progressive rate exceeds the standard rate
  2. Yes - progressive rates are always lower
  3. Yes - allowances always produce a saving
  4. It makes no difference to the tax payable

Correct: A — No - the marginal progressive rate exceeds the standard rate.

Above roughly $200,000 of net chargeable income the marginal progressive rate is 17%, against a 15% standard rate. Aggregating the property income drags it up to 17%, and there is no mortgage interest or loss for the election to unlock. Compute both ways - never assume.

Q270. Which of the following cannot elect for personal assessment?

  2. A company

Q272. A married couple elect for personal assessment. What allowance may they claim between them for their marital status?

  1. Both allowances in full, one after the other
  2. Neither allowance, since personal assessment has its own separate scale
  3. Half of each allowance
  4. The higher of the two allowances - not both

Q274. Are the concessionary deductions available under salaries tax (self-education expenses, home loan interest, elderly residential care expenses, MPF contributions, approved charitable donations) also available under personal assessment?

  1. No concessionary deductions are available under personal assessment
  2. Yes - the same concessionary deductions (self-education expenses, home loan interest, MPF contributions, elderly residential care expenses, approved charitable donations, and so on) are all available
  3. Only home loan interest is available; the others are salaries-tax only
  4. Concessionary deductions are capped at half their normal limits under personal assessment

E · Personal assessment Computation Medium

Q275. Does electing personal assessment change how income from each individual source (property, employment, business) is itself computed?

  1. Yes - personal assessment recomputes every source of income on a cash receipts basis
  2. Only property income is recomputed; business profits keep their normal basis
  3. No - each source of income keeps its own basis; property income is still computed on an accruals basis and business profits on the normal profits tax basis, merely aggregated afterwards
  4. Personal assessment abolishes the concept of a basis period entirely

Correct: C — No - each source of income keeps its own basis; property income is still computed on an accruals basis and business profits on the normal profits tax basis, merely aggregated afterwards.

Personal assessment is purely a re-aggregation and re-rating mechanism. Each source's net assessable value, net assessable income or assessable profits is computed exactly as it would be under the ordinary property tax, salaries tax or profits tax rules, and only then are the results added together and taxed afresh.

E · Personal assessment When it helps Hard

Q276. In which of the following is personal assessment LEAST likely to produce any tax saving?

  1. A property is solely owned by one spouse and let for the whole year
  2. A sole trader's business runs at a loss for the year
  3. A taxpayer pays substantial home loan interest with little other income to absorb allowances
  4. A property is jointly owned by a married couple; only the spouse who elects personal assessment includes their own share

Correct: D — A property is jointly owned by a married couple; only the spouse who elects personal assessment includes their own share.

Where a property is solely owned and let for the whole year with no mortgage, there is nothing for personal assessment to unlock - no loss to use, no interest to deduct beyond what property tax itself already sets off against rates, and the standard rate on that income alone is likely to beat the progressive computation once it is aggregated with other income. Always compute the tax both ways rather than assuming.

E · Personal assessment Practical approach Medium

Q277. In an exam question asking whether a married couple should elect for personal assessment, what should the answer actually compare?

  1. The whole family's tax position, computed both with and without the election, for each spouse separately and jointly
  2. Only the spouse with the higher income
  3. The election is never worth comparing - it should always be made if available
  4. Only the spouse who owns the let property

Correct: A — The whole family's tax position, computed both with and without the election, for each spouse separately and jointly.

Compute the total family tax bill under each realistic combination - no election, each spouse electing separately, and a joint election - and recommend whichever gives the lowest total. There is no shortcut rule that always wins; election is a comparison exercise, not an automatic saving.

E · Personal assessment Business losses Hard

Q278. A sole trader's business made a loss last year (say YA 2024/25), which was simply carried forward under the profits tax computation because no election was made at the time. Can personal assessment still be used now to relieve that loss against last year's other income?

  1. The loss is forfeited once the year of assessment for the loss has passed
  2. The loss is still relievable, since personal assessment for a year can be elected up to two years after that year ends
  3. The loss must be relieved in the same year it is incurred or never at all
  4. Only losses incurred in the current year of assessment qualify

Correct: B — The loss is still relievable, since personal assessment for a year can be elected up to two years after that year ends.

The time limit for electing personal assessment for a given year runs for two years after that year of assessment ends (or longer following a late assessment), so there is still time to go back and elect for YA 2024/25 itself, setting that year's loss against that year's other income, rather than only being able to use the loss against future profits.

Q279. In the personal assessment computation, what does 'total income' mean before it becomes 'reduced total income'?

  1. It is the same thing as net chargeable income
  2. It is total income after personal allowances only
  3. It is the total income of the individual, before deducting interest, concessionary deductions or losses - the starting point of the computation
  4. It is only the individual's salary income, excluding property and business income

Correct: C — It is the total income of the individual, before deducting interest, concessionary deductions or losses - the starting point of the computation.

Total income is simply the sum of the individual's net assessable value, net assessable income and assessable profits from all sources - the starting point, before any of the personal-assessment-specific deductions (interest, concessionary deductions, losses) are subtracted to arrive at reduced total income.

E · Personal assessment Partnership interaction Medium

Q280. An individual partner in a mixed partnership elects personal assessment. What happens to the corporate partner's share of the same partnership's profit?

  1. It is excluded from the partnership computation entirely
  2. It is halved
  3. It is deferred until the corporate partner also elects personal assessment
  4. It is included in full, since a corporate partner's own separate assessment is unaffected by what an individual partner elects

Correct: D — It is included in full, since a corporate partner's own separate assessment is unaffected by what an individual partner elects.

Each partner's share is assessed independently of what any other partner elects. The corporate partner's share of profit continues to be assessed under ordinary profits tax at the corporate rate, entirely unaffected by the individual partner's personal assessment election.

E · Personal assessment Eligibility Medium

Q281. Must a taxpayer have more than one source of chargeable income before personal assessment is worth considering?

  1. Yes - a non-Hong Kong-sourced loss cannot be brought in, but Hong Kong-sourced property, employment and business income can all still be aggregated even where some of it individually would be modest
  2. No - personal assessment requires at least two separate sources of chargeable income
  3. No - personal assessment is only available where property income exists
  4. Yes, but only where the taxpayer's total income exceeds $1,000,000

Correct: A — Yes - a non-Hong Kong-sourced loss cannot be brought in, but Hong Kong-sourced property, employment and business income can all still be aggregated even where some of it individually would be modest.

There is no such requirement in the legislation - even a taxpayer with a single source can elect - but as a matter of practical benefit, personal assessment tends only to help where there is property or business income (with a loss, or mortgage interest, or no allowances of its own) to combine with something else; a single salary source alone gains nothing from electing.

Q282. What happens if an election for personal assessment is made after the statutory time limit in s.41(2) has passed?

  1. A late election is always accepted with no discretion required
  2. The Commissioner may refuse to accept a late election, though in practice a reasonable excuse for the delay is usually accommodated
  3. A late election converts automatically into an objection instead
  4. There is no consequence, since personal assessment has no time limit at all

Correct: B — The Commissioner may refuse to accept a late election, though in practice a reasonable excuse for the delay is usually accommodated.

The election is out of time and the Commissioner is not obliged to accept it, though there is administrative practice of accepting a late election where there is a reasonable explanation - it should never be assumed as of right.
