Business — Profits Tax

Profits Tax by Entity

The same charge under s.14, but three materially different computations: sole proprietorship, partnership, and corporation. The partnership carries allocation mechanics on top, and only individuals can carry their share into personal assessment.

1. Why the entity changes the computation

Profits tax has one charging section for all three. What differs is the rate, what counts as a deductible payment to the owner, and what happens after the assessable profit is struck.

The idea underneath all of it An unincorporated business is not separate from its owner. A sole proprietor cannot employ himself, and a partner cannot be an employee of his own partnership — so a "salary" to them is an appropriation of profit, not an expense. A company is a separate person, so it genuinely can employ its director and genuinely does pay them a salary.

Nearly every difference below follows from that one point. Once you see it, you do not have to memorise the table — you can derive it.

The second consequence is who bears the tax. A company pays its own profits tax and the director separately pays salaries tax on the salary. A sole proprietor's profit is taxed once, in the business — but the proprietor may then elect personal assessment to have it re-taxed at progressive rates after allowances. A company cannot elect at all.

s.17(2) reaches the spouse, and that surprises people A salary to a sole proprietor's or partner's spouse is not deductible even where the spouse works full time for a commercial wage. Worse, the spouse is still chargeable to salaries tax on what they receive — so the same money is taxed in the business and again in the spouse's hands. The equivalent payment in a company is deductible. This is one of the sharper practical differences between the forms.

3. Sole proprietorship

One computation, one taxpayer. The whole assessable profit belongs to the proprietor, and the only question after that is whether personal assessment improves on it.

Illustration

Fiona carries on a design studio as a sole proprietor. The accounts for the year ended 31 March 2027 show a profit of $960,000, after charging a salary of $360,000 to Fiona, a salary of $180,000 to her husband (who works full time in the business at a commercial wage), and Fiona's own mandatory MPF contributions of $18,000. Depreciation allowances are $80,000.

Then two further consequences that are easy to forget:

The husband is still taxed His $180,000 is disallowed to the business but remains his employment income. Salaries tax: $180,000 − $145,000 basic allowance = $35,000 net chargeable income, at 2% = $700. The money is taxed twice.
Personal assessment is available — but here it is worse Fiona could elect PA on the $1,420,000. Net chargeable income would be $1,275,000, giving $198,750 at progressive rates (the standard-rate comparison on $1,420,000 gives $213,000, so progressive wins). That is well above the $106,500 profits tax she already pays. Do not elect.
The general shape Personal assessment helps a sole proprietor whose profit is low — where allowances and progressive rates beat a flat 7.5% — or who has property income or a loss to bring in. At $1.4m of profit the 17% marginal rate is already above 7.5%, so it costs money. Compute both; never assume.

4. Partnership

One assessment is raised on the partnership, not on the partners. But the profit must still be allocated partner by partner — because the allocation drives loss relief, personal assessment, and (where a partner is a company) the rate itself.

StepWhat happens
1Compute the partnership's assessable profit or adjusted loss in the ordinary way, adding back salaries and interest to partners and to partners' spouses under s.17(2).
2Allocate prior allocations first — the salaries and interest just added back go to the partner they relate to, not into the profit-sharing ratio.
3Share the balance in the profit-sharing ratio. The balance is often negative once the prior allocations are taken out.
4Reallocate so that no partner is left on the wrong side of zero: where the partnership has assessable profits, a partner cannot keep a loss share, and vice versa.
5Apply the rates — by partner if their natures differ (§2b).
Step 4 is the one candidates miss If the partnership's bottom line is a profit, no partner may be allocated a loss: any negative share is reallocated to the profit-making partners in proportion. If the partnership's bottom line is a loss, the mirror applies — any partner allocated a profit has it reallocated to the loss-making partners. The June 2025 examiner's report names this explicitly.

A full worked partnership allocation, with a loss per the accounts that adjusts to a profit, is Question 9 on the ACCA TX-HKG page →

5. Partnerships with a corporate partner

A company can be a partner. When it is, the partnership contains partners taxed at different rates, and the two-tiered threshold has to be shared out.

The rule Each partner's share of the assessable profits is taxed at that partner's own rate — 8.25%/16.5% for a corporate partner, 7.5%/15% for an individual. And the $2,000,000 first-tier band is itself apportioned between the partners in their profit-sharing ratio. The partnership does not get one $2,000,000 band at a single rate.

The mixed partnership lands exactly between the two — which is the point. A single blended rate applied to the whole $3,000,000 would not produce $315,000, and computing the first tier once at either rate would be wrong for one of the partners.

Loss relief for a corporate partner Where the partnership makes a loss, a corporate partner's allocated share can be set against that company's own other assessable profits — the loss is not trapped in the partnership. An individual partner's share goes into their personal assessment or is carried forward against future shares of the same partnership. The examining team noted that candidates were "not familiar with the loss relief for corporate partners".

The pattern, across both illustrations on this page Fiona at $1.42m and Carol at $1.5m both find personal assessment worse. That is not a coincidence. Business profits are taxed at 7.5% in an unincorporated business, and the progressive marginal rate reaches 17% once net chargeable income passes $200,000. For a profitable business with no other reliefs to unlock, PA is usually a cost.

It turns beneficial when there is something for it to unlock: mortgage interest on a let property, a business loss to set against salary, or allowances going unused because the income is not employment income. Those are the cases to look for — not size of profit.

7. Corporation

The standard case, and the one the general profits tax format is written for. The company is a separate person, so payments to its directors are real expenses.

Illustration — the same business as §3, incorporated

Fiona Ltd carries on the same design studio. The accounts show the same profit of $960,000 after the same salary of $360,000 to Fiona, now a director, and $180,000 to her husband, a genuine employee. Depreciation allowances are $80,000.

The company pays tax at the higher headline rate and still comes out $15,350 lighter, because $540,000 of remuneration moves out of the 7.5%-plus-nothing charge and into the progressive salaries tax bands, where the first $145,000 is covered by an allowance and the next slices are taxed at 2%–14%.

Read that as a structural point, not as advice The comparison isolates one variable to show how s.17(2) drives the difference. A real incorporation decision turns on much more — limited liability, audit and filing costs, business registration, stamp duty on any transfer of the business, how profits are actually extracted, and what happens on a later sale. The figures above are an illustration of a mechanism, not a recommendation. And the answer reverses at other profit levels: push the profit high enough and the 17% salaries tax band overtakes the 8.25% corporate rate.

9. Exam checklist

Before you startAsk
Which entity is it?Sole proprietorship, partnership or company. This sets the rate before anything else. Answering an unincorporated question at 8.25% loses the rate marks and every figure after them.
Is anyone paid who is the owner, or married to one?In an unincorporated business both are disallowed under s.17(2). In a company both are deductible if genuine.
Whose MPF is it?The proprietor's or partner's own mandatory contributions are deductible under s.16AA, capped at $18,000. Their spouse's are not.
Is there a corporate partner?Then the rates differ by partner and the $2,000,000 band is apportioned in the profit-sharing ratio.
Does anyone have a share on the wrong side of zero?Reallocate. No loss share when the partnership is in profit; no profit share when it is in loss.
Has the question asked about personal assessment?Only an individual can elect. Compute it both ways and say which is better — the answer is often "do not elect", and saying so with figures is the mark.
Is the spouse's disallowed salary still taxed?Yes, on the spouse. If the question gives their personal circumstances, it wants that computation too.
